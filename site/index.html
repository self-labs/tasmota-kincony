Why this exists

The published binaries do not fit these boards

Each of these three gaps is enough to leave a board half dead, and two of them fail without a single error message.

The full IR driver

The standard ESP32-S3 build has the basic one, which cannot pick an emitter: eight terminals behave as one. The IR preset has the full driver but drops 97 features to fit a 4 MB chip, SPI among them, and with it the Ethernet.

The PCF8574 expanders

The 16 relays and 16 inputs of the A16v3 sit behind I2C expanders, and that driver is commented out in every official build. The board boots with nothing to switch.

Ethernet without a USB cable

The W5500 of both boards shares two pins with the serial console. Without a USB host the console takes them and the link never comes up. The fix is in these builds, and was accepted upstream.

Install

Pick your board

Chrome or Edge on a desktop computer. Web Serial, which the installer needs, does not exist on Firefox, Safari, Android or iOS.

After flashing, six steps

Every command below goes in the board's own page: Tools → Console. Paste it, press Enter.

  1. Wi-Fi. Right after flashing, the installer asks for your network: fill it in and you are done here.
    • Skipped that screen? With no Wi-Fi saved, the board opens a network of its own, tasmota-XXXX. Join it from a phone or a laptop, open http://192.168.4.1 and enter your Wi-Fi there.
    • The installer says Disconnected? That is the board restarting: Tasmota restarts to join the network once it has the Wi-Fi, and on these boards the USB comes straight from the ESP32-S3, so every restart makes the port vanish from the browser. The Wi-Fi is already saved. Close it, wait 20 seconds and look for the board on your router or in Home Assistant.
  2. Template. Send this first, then Module 0 as a second command. The board restarts after Module 0. 
    Template {"NAME":"KinCony AG8","GPIO":[0,5536,5600,0,0,0,0,0,0,1056,1057,1058,1059,1060,1061,0,0,0,0,0,0,1062,0,0,0,0,0,0,0,0,5568,736,704,672,0,0,1063,1088],"FLAG":0,"BASE":1}
    Module 0
  3. Ethernet. 8 is the W5500 chip on this board. It restarts by itself, and after that the cable works without USB. 
    EthType 8
  4. Learning any remote. Without this, a remote whose protocol Tasmota does not know is ignored; with it, its raw timings come through and can be learned. 
    SetOption58 1
  5. Protection and clock. One line, three settings: 
    Backlog SetOption65 1; SetOption36 0; Timezone -3
    • SetOption65 1: seven quick power cycles no longer erase every setting.
    • SetOption36 0: five or six quick restarts no longer clear the template and the module.
    • Timezone -3: Brazil time. Tasmota ships at UTC+1; put your own offset instead of -3.
  6. Home Assistant. Add Tasmota IR from HACS: learned keys become buttons, air conditioners become climate entities.

The infrared side of this board is handled by Tasmota IR, which has its own documentation.

After flashing, seven steps

Every command below goes in the board's own page: Tools → Console. Paste it, press Enter.

  1. Wi-Fi. Right after flashing, the installer asks for your network: fill it in and you are done here.
    • Skipped that screen? With no Wi-Fi saved, the board opens a network of its own, tasmota-XXXX. Join it from a phone or a laptop, open http://192.168.4.1 and enter your Wi-Fi there.
    • The installer says Disconnected? That is the board restarting: Tasmota restarts to join the network once it has the Wi-Fi, and on these boards the USB comes straight from the ESP32-S3, so every restart makes the port vanish from the browser. The Wi-Fi is already saved. Close it, wait 20 seconds and look for the board on your router or in Home Assistant.
  2. Template. Send this first, then Module 0 as a second command. The board restarts after Module 0. 
    Template {"NAME":"KinCony A16v3","GPIO":[0,5536,5600,0,4704,4707,4705,4706,1152,640,608,0,0,0,0,5568,9408,9440,1120,0,0,0,0,0,0,0,0,1314,0,0,1376,736,704,672,0,0,1312,1313],"FLAG":0,"BASE":1}
    Module 0
    • The template already puts a WS2812 LED strip on GPIO41, pin 3 of the P12 header beside the ESP32-S3 (39 40 41 GND 3V3). With no strip wired there, the light it creates simply lights nothing.
    • The display stays off on purpose. Turning it on takes an Option A3 mark in the template, and the display then claims a power slot of its own: after the 16 relays it becomes power 17, the strip light moves to 18 and stops answering in Home Assistant. On a board without the strip, mark GPIO45 as 6210 and the display comes on.
  3. Ethernet. 8 is the W5500 chip on this board. It restarts by itself, and after that the cable works without USB. 
    EthType 8
  4. The two files. Download pcf8574.dat and display.ini, upload both under Tools → Manage File system, then restart: 
    Restart 1
  5. Inputs. Every input toggles the relay with the same number, on the board itself, and all 16 show up in Home Assistant next to the relays. One line of settings, then each rule on a line of its own, then one line that turns both rules on: 
    Backlog SetOption114 1; SwitchMode0 2
    Rule1 ON Switch1#State DO Power1 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch2#State DO Power2 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch3#State DO Power3 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch4#State DO Power4 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch5#State DO Power5 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch6#State DO Power6 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch7#State DO Power7 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch8#State DO Power8 TOGGLE ENDON
    Rule2 ON Switch9#State DO Power9 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch10#State DO Power10 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch11#State DO Power11 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch12#State DO Power12 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch13#State DO Power13 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch14#State DO Power14 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch15#State DO Power15 TOGGLE ENDON ON Switch16#State DO Power16 TOGGLE ENDON
    Backlog Rule1 1; Rule2 1
    • SetOption114 1: an input no longer drives a relay by itself. Every change still reaches the rules and goes out at once, and Tasmota announces the inputs, so Home Assistant creates them as binary sensors.
    • SwitchMode0 2: a closed contact reads on and an open one off. A rule with no value, like Switch1#State, fires on both, so every flip of a wall switch toggles the relay, and the switch position never has to match the relay when Home Assistant switches it too. In mode 0, Home Assistant would only offer the inputs as automation triggers.
    • The rules live on the board and run there, with no Home Assistant and no network, and nothing toggles at boot: the inputs are read as they are when Tasmota starts and ignored for the first 4 seconds. Send each rule on its own line, never inside a Backlog, which splits on ;. They take 319 and 333 of the 511 characters a rule holds, and Rule3 stays free for anything else the house needs.
    • Per channel, change that input's part and send the whole rule again: another relay is another number after Power; a pushbutton, the kind that springs back, keeps mode 2 and uses Switch3#State=1, the press; an input that only informs Home Assistant, or a free one, is left out of the rule. PulseTime13 5 turns relay 13 off by itself after 0.5 s: 1 to 111 are tenths of a second, above that it is seconds plus 100, and 0 removes the pulse.

    Optional: input names. Home Assistant shows the inputs as Switch1 to Switch16. To have IN1 to IN16 instead, send this before the board joins Home Assistant, since the entity id is born from the first name. The rules keep calling them Switch1 to Switch16: 

    Backlog SwitchText1 IN1; SwitchText2 IN2; SwitchText3 IN3; SwitchText4 IN4; SwitchText5 IN5; SwitchText6 IN6; SwitchText7 IN7; SwitchText8 IN8; SwitchText9 IN9; SwitchText10 IN10; SwitchText11 IN11; SwitchText12 IN12; SwitchText13 IN13; SwitchText14 IN14; SwitchText15 IN15; SwitchText16 IN16

    Optional: relay names. Home Assistant takes the relay names from Tasmota, which ships with Tasmota, Tasmota2 and so on, and keeps only 8. The first line names relays 1 to 8 OUT1 to OUT8; send it before the board joins Home Assistant, like the input names. Relays 9 to 16 arrive as switch 9 to switch 16, and the strip as light 17: rename those in Home Assistant itself. The second line puts OUT1 to OUT16 on the relay buttons of the board's own page, and does not reach Home Assistant: 

    Backlog FriendlyName1 OUT1; FriendlyName2 OUT2; FriendlyName3 OUT3; FriendlyName4 OUT4; FriendlyName5 OUT5; FriendlyName6 OUT6; FriendlyName7 OUT7; FriendlyName8 OUT8
    Backlog WebButton1 OUT1; WebButton2 OUT2; WebButton3 OUT3; WebButton4 OUT4; WebButton5 OUT5; WebButton6 OUT6; WebButton7 OUT7; WebButton8 OUT8; WebButton9 OUT9; WebButton10 OUT10; WebButton11 OUT11; WebButton12 OUT12; WebButton13 OUT13; WebButton14 OUT14; WebButton15 OUT15; WebButton16 OUT16

    Variant: no rules. Tasmota itself ties each input to its relay, and Home Assistant shows only the relays: 

    Backlog SetOption114 0; SwitchMode0 0; Rule1 0; Rule2 0
    • SetOption114 0: input 1 switches relay 1, and so on up to 16, on the board itself, and Tasmota no longer announces the inputs. SwitchMode0 0 is for latching switches; SwitchMode1 3 gives input 1 a pushbutton.
    • Rule1 0; Rule2 0: the two rules are turned off, not erased.
    • Switching between the two removes or recreates the 16 input sensors in Home Assistant, along with any area or name given to them there.
  6. Protection and clock. One line, three settings: 
    Backlog SetOption65 1; SetOption36 0; Timezone -3
    • SetOption65 1: seven quick power cycles no longer erase every setting.
    • SetOption36 0: five or six quick restarts no longer clear the template and the module.
    • Timezone -3: Brazil time. Tasmota ships at UTC+1; put your own offset instead of -3.
  7. LED strip. Only with a strip on GPIO41. One line, two settings: 
    Backlog Pixels 7; Dimmer 30
    • Pixels 7: how many LEDs the strip has. Tasmota starts at 30; put your own count.
    • Dimmer 30: 30% brightness. The strip draws from the board's 5 V, the same rail as the ESP32, so keep it low until you have measured that 5 V with the strip lit.

Both files come from this repository. pcf8574.dat turns the 32 expander pins into relays and inputs; display.ini describes the SSD1306 and is only read on a board that turns the display on (step 2).

Close the installer's window first: only one page can hold the port. A board that was already running from 12 V before the USB went in does not answer over USB; the second button restarts it so it does, and the relays go back to where they were.

Back the board up first

Installing writes the factory image at offset 0: bootloader, partition table, application and safeboot. On a board coming from another firmware the installer first asks whether to erase the flash, and the box starts unticked. Coming from KCS, tick it: that is what makes the board boot clean, and it also erases KCS along with everything configured and learned inside it.

The KCS firmware itself is not lost. KinCony publishes it per board on their forum, and the file inside the zip is a complete image, bootloader and partition table included, so it goes back at offset 0 the same way. What no download brings back is what the board held: the settings, the scenes and the IR codes learned into the KCS slots. A browser cannot read the flash, and that is what the backup is for. 

python -m esptool --chip esp32s3 --port COM3 --baud 460800 read_flash 0x0 0x1000000 backup-16MB.bin

The file must be exactly 16,777,216 bytes, and write_flash 0x0 backup-16MB.bin puts the board back the way it came, settings and learned codes included.

How it works

Three minutes, start to finish

Plug the board in

USB-C, with a cable that carries data. If the board is not listed, hold DW, tap RST, release DW, and try again.

Click install

The browser writes the factory image straight from this page, asking first whether to erase the flash. Nothing is installed on your computer.

Join your Wi-Fi

The installer offers to set Wi-Fi up over the same cable. From there the board has its own web interface, and Home Assistant finds it by MQTT.

Where the firmware comes from

Tasmota, plus two environments

Both builds come from cateim/Tasmota, branch tasmota-kincony: Tasmota itself with one environment per board. Every push there builds both and publishes a release, and this page serves the binaries of the latest one.

Updating Tasmota

Merge the upstream release into that branch and push. Only two files carry the changes, so that is the whole update, and the site picks the result up within a day.

Updating a board

The page recognises a board that already runs one of these builds, by the name it announces (Tasmota AG8, Tasmota A16v3), and offers Update, written without erasing, and the settings are kept: tested on the A16v3. Backing the configuration up first (Configuration, Backup) still costs nothing. Uploading the plain .bin under Firmware Upgrade, in the board's own web interface, does the same from the board itself.

Reading the source

Tasmota is GPL-3.0 and the branch is public, which is the complete source of what this page writes onto your board.

FAQ

Before you click

Can I go back to the KinCony firmware?

Yes, and you do not need the backup for the firmware itself. KinCony publishes KCS per board on their forum, free and without an account, as one zip per board (KCS_AG8_*.bin, KCS_A16V3_*.bin). Each is a complete image with bootloader and partition table, so write_flash 0x0 with the file from the zip puts the board back on KCS. The board comes back blank, though: the settings, the scenes and the IR codes learned into the KCS slots live in the board's own storage, and only the backup taken before flashing brings those back.

Does it work from a phone or tablet?

No. Flashing needs Web Serial, which only Chrome and Edge on a desktop have. Once the board is flashed, its web interface and Home Assistant work from any browser.

Are my settings kept when I flash from here?

On a board already running one of these builds, yes: it is offered Update, written without erasing, and the settings are kept, as tested on the A16v3. Anything else, KCS included, gets a fresh install, and the installer asks first whether to erase; coming from KCS, tick it. Either way, a configuration backup (Configuration, Backup) takes a second and restores everything at once.

Where is my board on the network?

Find my board, on this page, asks it over USB and shows both addresses. By hand, press Install on the board's card, pick the port, open Logs & Console, type Status 5 and press Enter. In the answer, IPAddress is the Wi-Fi address and the one inside "Ethernet" is the cable's; 0.0.0.0 means that side is not connected. The board only listens on USB when the cable was already plugged in as it started: if it was powered from 12 V first, the log says no SOF packet detected on USB port. Restart it with the USB plugged in, by the reset button or by cutting the power, and ask again.

The serial log says "SHA-256 comparison failed". Is it broken?

No. The lines right after it say Attempting to boot anyway..., and the board does. The chip's ROM checks a SHA-256 stored at the end of the bootloader before starting it, and without secure boot a mismatch is only a warning. Builds from before 24 September 2026 wrote a bootloader that declared 4 MB of flash; on its first boot Tasmota corrects that byte to the real 16 MB (resize_max_flash_size_k in partition_core.be) and leaves the hash as it was, so the ROM prints this on every boot from then on. The Expected hash in the log is the one for the 4 MB header, and the Calculated one is the same bootloader with 16 MB. Current builds declare 16 MB from the start, with a matching hash, so a board installed or updated from this page stops showing it. An update over the air replaces only the application and keeps the old bootloader, and the message with it, which is harmless.

Does the board phone home?

No. Tasmota talks to your MQTT broker and to whatever you point it at. The over the air update URL is deliberately left empty in these builds, so an accidental upgrade cannot replace them with a firmware that lacks the drivers these boards need.
